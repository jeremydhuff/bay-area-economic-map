Bay Area as a share of US GDP

San Francisco, San Mateo, Santa Clara, Santa Cruz, Alameda, Contra Costa, Solano, Napa, Sonoma and Marin. The rise is real but it is not a straight line: the region reached 2.1% by 1880, lost ground for half a century, and did not pass that mark again until 1933. Everything before 2001 is estimated and everything after 2024 is a nowcast.

The decomposition

A share of output is an identity: share of GDP = share of people × income per person, relative to the U.S. The whole modern rise is the second term.

Share of U.S. population

Ten counties as a share of U.S. residents. It peaked in 1990 and has fallen since — none of the modern rise in output share is people.

Decennial census counts to 2020; Census Bureau annual estimates after.

Income per person vs the U.S.

Bay Area income per head divided by U.S. income per head. It fell for 120 years, bottomed at 1.25× in 1973, and has doubled since.

Derived: GDP share ÷ population share. 1.0× is the national average.

Population of the ten counties

~48,000 people in 1852 and 7.9 million now — but the region has added 859,000 residents since 2000, fewer than it added in the single decade of the 1940s, and it is smaller today than at the 2020 census.

Methodology

The series is stitched from five regimes. Each is estimated on the best data that exists for its period, and each is pinned to the next so the joins are continuous rather than assumed. Every figure is nominal — a share of current-dollar GDP — which is the convention BEA uses for metropolitan shares.

2001–2024 · measured

BEA's county GDP accounts begin in 2001. The ten counties are summed and divided by national GDP. Nothing is estimated.

st= ∑cYc,t YtUS

BEA's all-industry U.S. total equals NIPA GDP exactly, so no reconciliation is needed.

2025–2026 · nowcast

County output for 2025 will not be published until December 2026. Split the share into the Bay Area's share of California (β) and California's share of the country (γ).

s^t= βt·γt

γ is observed quarterly and it is nearly flat: 13.87%, 13.81%, 14.05%, 13.96%, 13.95%, 13.91% for the six quarters from 2025 Q1 to 2026 Q2. California grew 6.00% in nominal terms in 2025; the United States grew 5.04%.

That is the binding constraint. If the AI build-out has raised the Bay Area's share of national output, it has done so entirely by taking share from the rest of California — because California in total has not gained an inch on the country. Everything rests on β, which cannot be observed for either year.

1969–2000 · spliced on personal income

County income runs back to 1969, thirty-two years further than county output. Write pt for the Bay Area's share of U.S. personal income and κt for the ratio of the output share to the income share. In 2001, where both are observed, κ = 1.011.

s^t= κt·pt

The two differ because output is booked where it is produced and income where the earner lives. That wedge has widened since 2001, to 1.09 by 2024, which is a reason to think it was slightly narrower before 1969 — so this segment may run a little high.

1929–1968 · population × relative income

No county income data exist. But an income share is an identity: the population share times relative income per person. πt comes from the census; ρt is California's per-capita income against the country from BEA state accounts, which carries the Depression and the war; φ is the Bay Area's premium over California.

s^t= πt·ρt·φ

φ is not guessed: it is set to whatever value makes this segment meet the spliced segment exactly at 1969, which gives 1.10.

1850–1928 · population × Easterlin

The same identity, with Easterlin's estimates of Pacific-division income per head (204% of the U.S. average in 1880, 135% in 1920, 120% in 1950) standing in for ρ, scaled by the Bay Area's premium over the division, ψ.

s^t= πt·ψ·et

ψ is calibrated the same way φ was — chosen so this segment meets the next one at 1929 — and comes out at 1.20. That is a check, not a fit: Easterlin's 1920 Pacific figure carried forward nine years lands within about one percent of the level BEA's 1929 California accounts imply, using a Bay-area premium of a plausible size. Two independent bodies of work agree at the seam.

Before 1880 there is no benchmark at all, so the 1852 anchor is set by hand at 3.5× and given a wide band. As a sanity check on the order of magnitude: California's gold output in 1852 alone was about $81m against U.S. GDP of $3.1bn — 2.6% of the national economy dug in one year in a state whose entrepôt, bank and workshop was San Francisco.

Uncertainties
  1. 2025–26 is a scenario, not a measurement. It rests on one unobservable parameter, and the plausible range at the far right (4.5–5.0%) is nearly as wide as the whole rise since 2010.
  2. 1850 is the weakest point on the chart. The 1850 federal returns for San Francisco, Contra Costa and Santa Clara were destroyed; the figure used here is the 1852 California state census. Read the first decade as an order of magnitude.
  3. The war is smoothed away. Bay Area population between census years is log-interpolated, so the 1942–45 shipbuilding surge at Richmond and Sausalito — and the partial exodus after — never appears. The true 1943–44 share was probably a few tenths of a point above the line.
  4. Nominal shares flatter the 19th century. Prices on the Pacific coast ran far above eastern prices — Coelho and Shepherd put the gap near 40% for 1851–1880. A real-output share would show a noticeably lower 1850s and 1860s.
  5. Population changes source at 2020. Decennial counts give way to Census annual estimates, ratio-spliced to the 2020 base; 2026 is extrapolated one year. The region lost 219,000 people between the 2020 census and 2022 and has recovered about a third.
  6. County boundaries move. San Mateo was carved out of San Francisco in 1856 and Alameda out of Contra Costa and Santa Clara in 1853, so the earliest county figures are partly retrospective reconstructions.
  7. Easterlin's Pacific division is not the Bay Area. It carries Oregon and Washington too. The ψ premium absorbs the difference in a single constant.
  8. Even the measured segment moves. BEA revises county GDP; the 2012 jump in San Mateo and the 2008 spike in Contra Costa are industry-reclassification artefacts as much as economics.
